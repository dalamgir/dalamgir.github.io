# AI is not the engineer, You are!

Imagine, what is now, a very real scenario - An engineer gets a task to add a new feature. They paste the requirements into their favorite AI coding assistant, get back a complete implementation, copy it into their codebase and ship it. The feature works. The CR is approved. But three months later, when a related bug appears, no one on the team - including the original author - can explain why the code works the way it does.

This isn’t a story about AI failing. Its about AI succeeding in all the wrong ways.

The most valuable skill in software engineering has never been cranking out millions of lines of code. It’s forming a clear mental model of a system that is yet to materialize. It’s dealing with ambiguity, making tradeoffs and converging on the right shape of the solution through many iterations. This process isn’t always pleasant or comfortable, but its the core of what software engineering is - complex problem solving. AI is exceptionally good at removing the discomfort from the process. And that’s exactly the problem. When used incorrectly, AI doesn’t just accelerate work - it short-circuits the path of structuring raw thoughts on a problem into a coherent solution. You may think that AI will steal your engineering job, but using it carelessly allows it to steal something way more valuable - the thinking that makes your job worth doing.

AI is powerful because, when used well, it can take the mechanical and repetitive tasks off the engineer’s plate so that they can spend more time on exploration, judgement, tradeoffs - the parts of software engineering that actually matter. The goal isn’t to replace thinking, but to augment and elevate it. AI should make it easier to be a thoughtful engineer, not easier to avoid thinking altogether.

AI doesn’t fail loudly when it’s misused - it fails quietly, over time. The output looks fine, progress feels fast, and nothing immediately breaks - instant gratification. What gets diluted is the process that makes an engineer effective in the first place. Software engineers need to retain the core essence of software engineering while accommodating AI in a way which augments their skillset and boosts productivity. In order to achieve this, its critical to understand how to **_*not*_** use AI.

**Using AI to tackle ambiguity too early**. Complex problems require dealing with significant ambiguity and sometimes the ambiguity lingers. It takes many iterations of deliberating about the problem, the requirements, the design before settling on a potential solution. This requires careful considerations of many factors. When AI is used to “resolve” ambiguity, it often forces a prematurely shaped solution on to the problem - a solution which hasn’t been earned yet. The result can look clean but its built on shallow understanding.

**Outsourcing your thinking**. One thing every strong engineer brings to the table is strong opinions. These opinions are shaped by their internal narratives of how a system works or should work. These opinions are the ingredients for immersive design, architecture and implementation discussions which allows a team to arrive at the necessary solutions. When AI provides the explanation before you’ve built your own, the internal narrative never fully forms. You may be able to repeat the words, but you’ve lost the ability to reason through them.

**Letting AI remove friction that could’ve been a learning experience**. Friction can reveal itself in the form of confusing code, awkward abstractions, or really anything that slows the progress whether thats understanding code, debugging an issue or monitoring a system. This type of friction is almost always looked at as a defect, but is actually often a signal. When AI smooths over that friction too quickly, it can hide structural problems behind plausible output. It might have allowed you to move forward, but with less insight than you should have gained.

**Mistaking AI fluency for technical depth**. Knowing how to prompt an LLM is not the same as knowing what good software looks like. Technical depth is built through exposure, mistakes and most importantly, reflection. AI can produce many options, but it can’t tell you which one aligns with long-term maintainability and the evolution of the product or system. If you don’t cultivate technical depth, you won’t know what to keep or discard.

**Copy-pasting output you couldn’t reconstruct**. This one is the most obvious one. Everyone knows this at the back of their mind but its worth an exclusive mention because of how deceptively costly it can be. Code you can’t mentally re-derive is code you don’t truly own. When requirements change or edge cases appear, the lack of ownership becomes a liability. The fastest way to slow a team down is to fill the codebase with artifacts that no one fully understands.

AI is sufficiently powerful and an inevitable part of our daily lives as software engineers. However, we need to stop treating it like a magical shortcut that somehow exempts us from thinking. Used poorly, AI makes engineering feel easier while quietly making it worse. However, when used well, it can do the opposite. So rather than arguing whether engineers should use AI, the more interesting question is _***how***_\*\* \*\* \*\*to use it without turning ourselves into freeloaders.

**Using AI to expand ambiguity**. Strong engineers resist the urge to converge too quickly. Used well, AI helps explore the problem space by generating multiple interpretations of the problem, edge cases, and alternatives. It can only do so if we as engineers use our capacity to reason and engage in a conversation. This can expose design options and tradeoff potentials in an effort to keep solutions flexible longer and prevent early commitments to wrong abstractions. AI should widen the search before it narrows the path.

**Articulate the thinking, stress test with AI**. The value isn’t in the answer AI gives, but in how it challenges the answer you might potentially already have. By writing down your approach first, you force yourself to form a clear mental model. AI then becomes a tool to stress test the idea, poke holes, surface edge cases and expose weak assumptions. This improves the rigor of the process but more importantly, it preserves ownership. The solution was always yours.

**Using AI to interrogate friction, not erase it**. When something feels hard, that friction often contains information about the problem space. But not all friction is created equal. _Good friction_ signals genuine complexity: the API design is reflecting an inefficient domain model; the tests are hard to write because the architecture has undesired coupling; feature has a complicated implementation because some other requirement creates a contradiction. _Bad friction_ is just tooling overhead: boilerplate code, repetitive code etc. AI is excellent at removing bad friction - let it handle that. When you encounter “good” friction, resist the urge prompt your way through it. Instead, use AI to help you understand _why_ things are hard. This should train your intuition - detecting a problem deserves some explicit analysis, not automation.

**Acquiring technical depth through comparison, not delegation**. AI is excellent at generating variations, alternatives and competing designs. You need to be the reviewer. Reviewing and comparing these options sharpens your sense of what’s good, what’s brittle and what will not stand the test of time. Over time, this repeated evaluation builds intuition that no single generated answer can provide. Technical depth is built through experience and technical taste is built through choice, not blind acceptance.

Now if you’re an engineer early in your career and you’re still building your foundation, this may seem a little counter intuitive: how do you form strong opinions when you’re still learning what good opinions look like?

The answer isn’t to avoid AI - it’s to use it as a learning accelerator and not a shortcut. When AI generates code, don’t just accept it. You can ask the question - _what would I need to know to write this myself?_ Then use AI to learn those prerequisites. Ask it to explain the design patterns it used, the tradeoffs it made and the alternatives it rejected. The goal isn’t to match AI’s output or speed - it’s to compress your learning curve. Every AI-generated solution should leave you more capable than you were before you saw it.

**Treating AI output as your own code**. Anything AI produces should be something you can explain, modify and justify under scrutiny. This creates a natural quality bar that filters out shallow or inadequate solutions. When you can re-derive the result, you actually own it, even if you didn’t write it in the first place. AI accelerates the work, but responsibility remains human. The responsibility remains yours.

AI is one of the most powerful productivity tools software engineering has ever seen. It can make it easier to explore ideas that once took weeks or months to validate to mere days or even hours. Used intentionally, it supercharges an engineer’s productivity and expands what they can reasonable take on. But productivity without understanding is a fragile win. When AI is used to bypass judgement, curb thinking or avoid discomfort, it quietly erodes the very skills that make engineers valuable in the first place.

The engineers who thrive in this new world won’t be those who resist AI or those who surrender to it - they’ll be those who can figure out how to use AI as a force multiplier for understanding. This means developing a new skill: the ability to maintain ownership over work you did not produce. It means treating every AI interaction as a choice about what kind of engineer you want to become. AI will only get better from here at removing friction, resolving ambiguity and generating solutions. Your job is to get better at knowing when to let it - and when to insist on doing the thinking yourself. The real risk isn’t that AI replaces the engineers. It’s that engineers replace themselves by relinquishing the one thing that makes them irreplaceable: ownership of the problem, the solution and everything in between.
